# The Crew — Field Manual

A learning guide to the co-operative trick-taking series by Thomas Sing
(KOSMOS / Thames & Kosmos): the shared engine, the original rules, and the
current state of knowledge on *Journey to the Ends of the Earth* (2026).

## The engine — what every Crew game is [Solid]

A normal trick-taking game where the whole table wins or loses together, and
you are barred from talking about your hand.

### The deck

40 cards: 36 number cards in four colours numbered 1–9, plus 4 trump cards
numbered 1–4. The theme renames trump each time (rockets in space, submarines
under the sea) but its job never changes: trump beats any colour, and trump 4
is the strongest card in the game.

### A trick, step by step

1. The lead player plays any card. Its colour is the led colour.
2. Everyone else, clockwise, **must follow the led colour if they hold it**.
3. Holding none of it, they may play anything, trump included.
4. Highest card of the led colour wins — unless trump was played, in which
   case the highest trump wins.
5. The winner takes the cards and leads the next trick.

### Why it's hard

Winning tricks is not the goal. The right person must win the right card at
the right time. Most good plays are deliberate losses that set up a teammate —
and you cannot say so.

## Game one — The Quest for Planet Nine (2019)

Fifty story missions played in order. Tasks are concrete: *somebody must win
this exact card.*

### Setup [Solid]

- Deal the entire deck out (10 cards each at four players, 8 at five).
- Whoever holds the **trump 4** is the Commander and leads the first trick.
- The logbook mission states how many tasks to deal and any special restriction.
- Deal that many task cards face up. Starting with the Commander, players take
  turns claiming one task each until all are claimed.

### Tasks [Solid]

Each task card pictures one specific number card; whoever claims it must
personally win the trick containing that card. Every task must be completed —
one failure loses the mission for everybody.

You lose the moment a task becomes impossible (the named card goes to the
wrong player, or an ordering rule breaks). Don't play the hand out — reshuffle
and retry.

| Token | What it demands |
| --- | --- |
| 1 · 2 · 3 | These tasks must be completed in this exact numbered order. |
| Arrows | Relative order only — sequenced among themselves; other tasks may land anywhere around them. |
| Omega | This task must be the very last one completed in the mission. |

[Check the PDF] Tokens are dealt alongside task cards in matching order — first
token with the first task card. Missions specify which tokens are in play.

### Communication — the one valve [Solid]

Once per mission, each player may reveal exactly one card face up with their
communication token on it. Position states one fact:

| Token position | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Top | This is my **highest** card of that colour. |
| Middle | This is my **only** card of that colour. |
| Bottom | This is my **lowest** card of that colour. |

- You may only communicate a card that genuinely is your highest, lowest, or
  only one of its colour.
- **You may never communicate a trump card.**
- Communicate between tricks, not mid-trick.
- The card stays face up and is played normally later.
- Beyond this, no hints — no sighing, no "hmm", no telling anyone what to lead.

### The two dials [Check the PDF]

A **distress signal** lets the crew redistribute cards once, and communication
can be dropped entirely for a much harder game. Missions state when each is
available; read the exact procedure from the rulebook.

### The campaign

Fifty missions in order, each with story text. Fail one and replay it until
it's beaten. Progress is tracked in the logbook.

## Game two — Mission Deep Sea (2021): what changed

Same deck, same trick-taking, same communication valve. One change to the
tasks, and it changes everything.

| Element | Planet Nine | Mission Deep Sea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trump | Rockets | Submarines |
| Tasks | Win this specific card | Abstract conditions — win no pink, win exactly two tricks, win two in a row |
| Task dealing | Fixed count per mission | Each task has a difficulty value; draw until the mission's target is met |
| Replay value | Fixed missions | Randomised task mixes — the same mission plays differently |
| Feel | Mostly a personal errand | Whole-table problems running the full hand |

The shift worth internalising: in Planet Nine your task is largely *yours* to
solve. In Deep Sea a task like "the crew wins no green cards" binds everyone at
once, so the table steers as one unit for the entire hand. That direction —
tasks as shared constraints rather than personal errands — is the thread
running into the 2026 game.

## Game three — Journey to the Ends of the Earth (2026)

A standalone third entry: a globe-trotting hunt for Charlemagne's lost
treasure, from Everest to the Amazon. Released mid-2026 at Gen Con.

### Confirmed across multiple sources [Solid]

- Standalone — not an expansion, needs nothing from the earlier boxes.
- Thomas Sing / Thames & Kosmos, roughly 30 minutes, ages 10+.
- Co-operative trick-taking with the same limited-communication core.
- A campaign of roughly 24–25 stages, each its own mission with fresh rules
  layered on, tracked via a logbook printed at the back of the rulebook.
- Tasks lean further into whole-team objectives — the Deep Sea direction,
  pushed harder.
- A *Globetrotter* mini-expansion exists alongside it.

### What could not be pinned down [Unknown]

- **Player count** — sources disagree between 2–5 and 3–5.
- **Exact stage count** — both 24 and 25 appear in coverage.
- **Deck composition and trump name** — the series has always used 36 colour
  cards plus 4 trump and renames trump per theme, but neither is confirmed for
  this edition. Don't assume they carry over unchanged.
- **Communication rules** — some restricted communication is certain; whether
  the token works exactly as before is not. Read this first; it's where
  sequels in this series most often change the maths.
- **The new mission mechanisms** — coverage promises "new mission mechanics"
  without naming them. This is the genuinely new content.

These gaps are left marked rather than filled with plausible-sounding rules.
For a game you're about to learn, a confident wrong rule is worse than a marked
gap — you'd play twenty stages on a bad assumption before noticing.

## How to actually learn this

### The order that works

1. Follow suit and read a trick until it's automatic.
2. Play one-task hands with no ordering tokens; get the feel of deliberately
   losing a trick.
3. Add the communication token — practise choosing *when*. Most crews burn it
   far too early.
4. Add ordering tokens. This is where crews start failing, and where the game
   gets good.
5. Move to whole-table tasks, where one constraint binds everyone.

### Four habits of a good crew

- **Hold the token.** Your one communication is worth most when the table
  already has information to combine it with.
- **Count the trump.** Four exist. Whoever holds the last one controls the
  endgame.
- **Think in voids.** The instant a player fails to follow a colour, everyone
  learns they're out of it. Free information — and you leak it too.
- **Lose on purpose.** Every card you win is a card a teammate can't.

### Where crews lose

- Winning a trick containing someone else's task card by accident.
- Completing an ordered task out of sequence — legal-looking play, instant loss.
- Leading a colour a teammate is void in, handing them a discard they didn't
  want yet.
- Talking. Tone of voice counts as talking.
